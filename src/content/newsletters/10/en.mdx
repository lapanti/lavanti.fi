When you pay for strawberries at the market with a ten-euro note, the purchase leaves no trace in anyone's database. You need not explain it to the seller, the bank, or the state. In Finland this is so self-evident it goes unsaid.

Yet cash is disappearing from daily life, replaced by the card. Card payments usually run through a US company. [According to ECB data compiled by Euronews](https://www.euronews.com/business/2026/05/01/europe-moves-to-break-visa-and-mastercards-grip-but-not-everyone-agrees), Visa and Mastercard handle 61% of euro-area card payments, and nearly all cross-border ones. In Finland, [practically all card payments run through a US processor](https://dawn.fi/uutiset/2026/06/24/digitaalinen-euro-eteni-euroopan-parlamentissa).

A European digital euro could change this and strengthen Europe's [digital independence](/en/blog/51/digital-independence-is-a-necessity/). But we need to make sure it becomes digital cash: something you can pay with offline, whose purchases no centralised system can see.

## Payments are going digital regardless

Payments are shifting towards digital anyway. What remains is deciding whose system we use, on what terms. In 2026 the answer is two US companies.

They work well, and few of us think about it at checkout. Still, from a resilience standpoint this is fragile. When a whole society's everyday payments rest on two providers governed by another country's law, that's a [security-of-supply question](/en/blog/82/security-of-supply-ends-at-the-cloud/), like electricity or fuel. For merchants, this appears as [merchant fees card companies charge](https://dawn.fi/uutiset/2026/06/24/digitaalinen-euro-eteni-euroopan-parlamentissa).

The digital euro would be central bank money in electronic form, alongside notes and coins, not replacing cash.

[Under the Commission's proposal](https://www.euronews.com/business/2026/05/01/europe-moves-to-break-visa-and-mastercards-grip-but-not-everyone-agrees), the digital euro could work online or offline. [Under Parliament's position](https://www.europarl.europa.eu/news/en/press-room/20260622IPR45912/digital-euro-meps-want-to-ensure-sovereignty-privacy-and-financial-stability), basic use would be free for consumers. [According to the ECB](https://www.euronews.com/business/2026/05/01/europe-moves-to-break-visa-and-mastercards-grip-but-not-everyone-agrees), banks would act as its service providers and be paid for doing so. European payments would get a shared base for banks and payment firms to build on. [Banks' worry about deposits shifting into central bank money](https://www.euronews.com/business/2026/05/01/europe-moves-to-break-visa-and-mastercards-grip-but-not-everyone-agrees) is fair; the planned personal holding limit answers it by keeping savings in the bank.

## Build privacy in from the start

I have more than a decade of experience building secure software and banking services, and that work teaches one thing: a system's privacy is decided at the design stage. If every payment runs through a server that logs who paid whom, and for what, that data exists, and sooner or later someone finds a new use for it. If privacy isn't built in, the promise of it is easy to break later; privacy built in from the start is far harder to undo, because the data was never collected in the first place.

That is why the digital euro should be judged by one test: can a centralised system see what you buy? Purchase histories can reveal things people would rather no one knew. The US retailer Target even [inferred customers' pregnancies from shopping habits](https://www.nytimes.com/2012/02/19/magazine/shopping-habits.html). No one should have to hand over such information just to pay for coffee. Privacy is a [fundamental right that must not be dismantled without justification](/en/blog/45/fundamental-rights-must-not-be-dismantled-without-justification/).

The European Parliament's Committee on Economic and Monetary Affairs adopted its digital-euro report on 23 June 2026, [by 43 votes to 14, with 1 abstention](https://www.europarl.europa.eu/news/en/press-room/20260622IPR45912/digital-euro-meps-want-to-ensure-sovereignty-privacy-and-financial-stability). Parliament's press release puts privacy alongside sovereignty and financial stability. The position includes fully free offline payments, free basic services, and a cap on fees between merchants and service providers. Privacy would be built in via technology such as zero-knowledge proofs that verify a payment without revealing the payer's details. On 9 July 2026, the plenary voted, [by 416 to 169, with 22 abstentions](https://howtheyvote.eu/votes/195719), to mandate talks with the Council, representing the member states.

Offline payment means resilience as well as privacy. Cash has always had one strength: it works even when the network doesn't. Digital cash moving device to device without touching a server keeps that strength.

If privacy becomes a bargaining chip in the negotiations instead, Europe gets a [new surveillance layer](/en/blog/54/chat-control-parliament-was-right/), not cash's digital equivalent.

## When will the digital euro's terms be decided?

The digital euro's terms are being decided in the autumn 2026 negotiations, though the digital euro itself is still to come. [Euronews reports](https://www.euronews.com/business/2026/05/01/europe-moves-to-break-visa-and-mastercards-grip-but-not-everyone-agrees) it could see everyday use in 2029, if the law passes in 2026. EU leaders have set exactly that as their goal. In autumn 2026, Parliament, the Council, and the Commission are negotiating the final text. Once passed, the law's basic choices are locked in.

Practical work is moving ahead too. In July 2026, the ECB [selected 36 payment providers for the digital-euro pilot](https://www.ecb.europa.eu/press/pr/date/2026/html/ecb.pr260714~8cd07d9d45.en.html), and [Suomen Pankki (the Bank of Finland) and OP Pohjola agreed to join it](https://www.suomenpankki.fi/fi/ajankohtaista/lehdistotiedotteet-ja-uutiset/uutiset/2026/suomen-pankki-ja-op-pohjola-kaynnistavat-yhteistyon-digitaalisen-euron-pilotissa/). It runs 12 months, starting in the second half of 2027.

Finland is taking part where implementation is being tested. But the pilot only tests what the law allows, and the law is being written in the autumn 2026 negotiations.

## Write offline payments and privacy into law

Finland should push for three things in the negotiations:

- Offline payment is written into the law as a core feature of the digital euro.
- Privacy is built into the technology so no centralised system sees individual purchases.
- Basic use stays free for consumers, and a cap is set on digital euro merchant fees, so that the digital euro brings genuine competition to payments.

On these terms, the digital euro would preserve what a ten-euro note has always given: you can pay for what you buy without anyone writing it down.
