As a software developer, I have watched up close as Rovio, Supercell, Remedy and Housemarque turned Finland into one of the most competitive game development countries in the world. I still remember playing Max Payne in the early 2000s and realising the game had been made in Espoo. The same story continues today. Finnish game studios make world-class products that are a significant Finnish cultural export. Yet many promising studios stall at the start, because the money runs out exactly when it is needed most.

A national games institute is a proposal to move the distribution of public support for the games industry from Business Finland to the Ministry of Education and Culture, and to spread it more widely than today across small and new companies. The question is which door the same money goes through. The proposal is especially good because its premise is to redirect support that already exists, without raising state spending.

## What Is a National Games Institute?

The task would move to an existing ministry. No new administrative body would be created. The model would follow Denmark's [NIMBI](https://www.nimbi.dk/en/about-nimbi) institute, founded in August 2024 under the Ministry of Culture, which describes itself as the first of its kind in Europe. The support would be redistributed within the same appropriation.

Behind the proposal is the observation that games are a significant Finnish cultural export, while the industry's funding instruments were built on the terms of traditional manufacturing. The same logic holds elsewhere in cultural policy. Culture is infrastructure, as I wrote about [the possibilities Kirkkonummi's House of Culture Lyyra opens up](/en/blog/49/lyyra-the-house-of-culture-opens-new-possibilities-and-points-the-way/).

## Why Does the Business Finland Model Not Suit the Games Industry?

Business Finland's support for the games industry is structurally concentrated on a few established companies. [According to a Yle investigation](https://yle.fi/a/74-20230014), one large company received a quarter of all Business Finland games funding over two years, while the floor for the smallest grant has risen considerably. New and small studios lack exactly the early-stage funding a growth company needs first.

Global success stories like Rovio would benefit most from sound general industrial policy, as Atte Harjanne [notes in his proposal](https://www.verdelehti.fi/2026/05/20/harjanne-suomeen-saatava-kansallinen-peli-instituutti/). The problem hits new and small studios hardest. When domestic early-stage funding falls short, the risk is that skilled developers and new graduates move abroad. I have [written before](/en/blog/57/ai-changes-what-developers-get-paid-for/) about how keeping talent decides competitiveness in the AI era, in games and across the software industry.

## How Does Spreading the Support Produce More?

A spread-out model would divide the same amount among many small and new studios instead of a few large grants. Some projects will inevitably fail, but by Harjanne's own estimate the successful ones would return tens or even hundreds of times the support. The estimate is the proposer's own and has not been independently modelled. A broad pool of applicants would also keep the whole game development ecosystem lively and stop expertise from draining abroad.

> "Some of the small companies will naturally fail, but for those that succeed the support returns dozens, even hundreds of times over, and a spread-out support model at the same time sustains a vibrant game development ecosystem."

*Atte Harjanne, MP (Greens), written question to the government, May 2026 ([Verde](https://www.verdelehti.fi/2026/05/20/harjanne-suomeen-saatava-kansallinen-peli-instituutti/), our translation).*

The model rests on the same logic as market-based venture capital. Most companies never deliver a meaningful return, but the few that succeed cover the cost of the whole portfolio many times over. The proposal's three principles are:

- **Spread**: support as many small projects as possible instead of a few large individual grants.
- **Target group**: support goes to small and new companies; established large companies are supported through other industrial policy where needed.
- **Return logic**: some projects fail, but the return from the successful ones covers the whole and keeps the ecosystem competitive.

## Who Has Proposed a National Games Institute?

Green MP Atte Harjanne put forward the national games institute in May 2026 and submitted a written question on it to the government, which the government must answer in writing ([Verde](https://www.verdelehti.fi/2026/05/20/harjanne-suomeen-saatava-kansallinen-peli-instituutti/)). A written question is an oversight tool and not a bill, so the proposal moves forward only through the government programme. It ties into the same political timetable as the 2027 parliamentary election, when the next government programme is written. The aim of this piece is to back and advance a well-founded structural fix that is already on the table.

Harjanne's proposal rests on the same diagnosis as [the games industry association Neogames' own election programme](https://neogames.fi/fi/eduskuntavaalit27/). Small and growing game companies need early-stage funding that the current structure does not provide. The whole industry shares that diagnosis.
