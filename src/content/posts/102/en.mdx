An incentive trap is a situation in which work adds little to take-home pay, so accepting a job does not necessarily pay. An unemployed person may, for example, take a two-week gig, and a large part of the wage disappears into reduced benefits. Even a short gig throws the benefits into disarray when they are recalculated.

Work must always pay. There are two ways to make sure of it: we can tighten the conditions for support, or we can change the structure that shrinks support as earnings accumulate. Basic income works the second way.

## What is an incentive trap?

An incentive trap is a situation, created by the structure of social security, in which extra work adds little to take-home pay. When wages rise, unemployment benefit, housing allowance and social assistance fall at the same time, and taxes come on top. The cause is a benefit structure that cuts support as income grows.

A 2024 [calculation by Kela](https://www.kela.fi/ajankohtaista/kelan-laskelma-sosiaaliturvan-leikkaukset-heikentavat-kannustimia-pienipalkkaiseen-ja-osa-aikaiseen-tyohon-kelan-tyottomyysturvaa-saavilla) (the Social Insurance Institution) shows how this feels in the wallet. For a single parent in Helsinki receiving social assistance, the participation tax rate rose above 60 per cent at a wage of 400 euros and to 85 per cent at a wage of 1,000 euros. The participation tax rate tells how large a share of the wage disappears in reduced benefits and taxes. A closely related concept is the effective marginal tax rate. In the example calculation, a wage of one thousand euros left about 150 euros in hand.

From the beginning of March 2026, the 150-euro earnings disregard in social assistance was [abolished](https://www.kela.fi/ajankohtaista/toimeentulotuen-uudistus-voimaan-muutokset-nakyvat-asiakkaiden-elamassa-lahikuukausina) for people over 18. Net wages now reduce social assistance from the very first euro.

> A benefit recipient who calculates whether a gig is worthwhile is calculating correctly. The fault is in the formula.

## Why do tighter conditions not remove incentive traps?

Tighter conditions increase the pressure to accept work, but support still falls by the same amount for every extra euro earned. Stricter conditions also require more monitoring, and the cause of the incentive trap remains. Rising income still cuts support, so the same calculation repeats every time an unemployed person considers a short gig or part-time work.

Conditions do have an effect. According to the statement the [VATT Institute for Economic Research](https://vatt.fi/-/vatt-lausunto-toimenpide-ehdotuksista-julkisen-talouden-sopeutustarpeeseen-vastaamiseksi-ja-pitkaan-jatkuneen-velkaantumiskehityksen-taittamiseksi-valtiovarainministeriolle) gave to the Ministry of Finance, most of this government term's benefit cuts have probably sped up employment. At the same time, tighter conditions can lead to poorer-matched jobs, and poverty may have increased.

The same incentive can be achieved with a structure in which work always increases income. There is Finnish evidence for this. According to [a study](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0047272726001210), the income disregard in housing allowance introduced in 2015 raised the share of unemployed people doing part-time work by 4.1–4.5 percentage points, and no sign was found of weaker full-time work later on. The result concerns housing allowance and part-time work, and it shows that unemployed people take gigs when support does not eat a small wage.

## How does basic income remove incentive traps?

Basic income would remove incentive traps because it would be paid without a means test and would not shrink as wages rise. In [the Greens' model](https://www.vihreat.fi/ohjelmat/sosiaaliturvapoliittinen-ohjelma/), basic social assistance would be abolished, and a tax-free, unified basic income of 600 euros would replace a large share of current benefits. A municipality-specific housing supplement of 100–400 euros would come on top, and income disregards of 400 euros would be restored to the cause-based benefits. Work would then always pay.

Basic income does not mean living in comfort. The [general support](https://www.kela.fi/yleistuki) paid as unemployment security by Kela is 37.21 euros a day, on average about 800 euros a month before tax, and many people receive housing allowance alongside it. Basic income and the municipality-specific housing supplement together would be of the same order of magnitude, not more. The difference is that work would add to income without the basic income shrinking.

Basic income suits a market economy, because the state does not decide on people's behalf what money is spent on. Freedom of choice stays with the individual. When basic income is paid without a means test, fewer income checks are needed. In the housing supplement and in the cause-based benefits, income would still matter. This is one of many ways in which it shows that the Greens are [genuine defenders of the market economy](/en/blog/62/greens-the-true-defenders-of-the-market-economy/).

The same structure would serve the gig worker, the part-time worker and everyone whose career is interrupted or changes direction. In [a working life reshaped by AI](/en/blog/87/will-ai-take-programmers-jobs/), we need a floor that does not punish a gig. A starting entrepreneur would benefit from the same floor, and it would be an excellent complement to [fixing the YEL](/en/blog/73/yel-must-not-raise-the-entrepreneurship-barrier/) (the self-employed person's pension insurance).

## What would a first-stage basic income cost?

According to [a calculation by the Parliamentary Information Service (*Eduskunnan tietopalvelu*)](https://www.vihreat.fi/wp-content/uploads/2026/06/Ensi-vaiheen-perustulo.pdf), a monthly basic income of 260 euros would weaken public finances by just under 800 million euros in a static estimate. The figure is the net effect once changes to the basic deduction and the earned-income deduction, and the reduced benefits, have been taken into account. It is the Greens' first-stage cost estimate. The basic income would be tax-free, and it would not be paid to pensioners.

The first stage would be financed by changing the basic deduction and the earned-income deduction.

The first step narrows the incentive trap but does not yet remove it. In social assistance, the 260 euros would be counted as income, and from daily allowances it would be deducted. Wages would not, however, reduce the basic income itself, so a smaller and smaller share of income would remain within the reach of shrinking benefits. Because the daily allowance would start 260 euros lower, the adjusted benefit would end at a lower level of wages. Within the adjustment, an extra euro would still reduce support as before.

According to the calculation, the share of low-income people in the population would fall by one percentage point, meaning about 55,500 fewer people on a low income. The calculation is static and does not estimate employment effects. In line with the model, basic income would be raised gradually to 600 euros after the first step.

> When the floor stays in place, you can take a gig without doing the sums.

I am a candidate for Parliament in Uusimaa. Finland must introduce a basic income, starting from the 260-euro model and expanding to 600 euros. In the age of AI, in a changing working life, we must lower the threshold to work and entrepreneurship, not raise it.
