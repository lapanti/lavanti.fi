The public sector means central government, municipalities, wellbeing services counties and social security funds, which include the earnings-related pension institutions. This page goes through the figures, revenue first and then spending. This money pays for pensions, health centre visits and school days. It comes from income taxes, value added tax on purchases and other charges. According to the Ministry of Finance (VM) autumn forecast, the public sector collects about €160bn in 2027 and spends about €174bn.

## What is the public sector?

In the national accounts, the public sector means central government, local government and social security funds. Local government covers municipalities and wellbeing services counties; the social security funds include the earnings-related pension institutions and other funds that run social insurance. Parishes are not part of the public sector, so church tax is not in the figures. The central government budget is only one part of the public sector economy.

Because the earnings-related pension institutions are included, earnings-related pension contributions show up in public revenue and earnings-related pensions in public spending.

## Where does the public sector's money come from?

According to forecasts, public sector revenue in 2027 is about €160bn. The largest items are income taxes, about €46bn, social security contributions, about €37bn, and value added tax, about €29bn. Together these three make up about 70% of revenue. The tax ratio in 2027 is forecast at 41.8%.

Public revenue in 2027, estimate (total €160.4bn):

- Income taxes €46.2bn, of which earned and capital income taxes €38.5bn and corporate income tax €7.7bn
- Social security contributions €36.8bn, of which earnings-related pension contributions €30.1bn
- Value added tax €28.8bn
- Sales and fee income €19.0bn, such as client fees and sales of services
- Property income €12.2bn, meaning interest and dividends
- Other taxes and income €17.4bn, of which for example energy taxes €4.0bn, alcohol and tobacco taxes €2.8bn and real estate tax €2.4bn

Value added tax is shown gross, before the VAT refunds to municipalities and wellbeing services counties. In the central government budget proposal for 2027, the net figure is about €25bn. The tax ratio is the share of taxes and tax-like payments in gross domestic product.

## Where does the public sector's money go?

According to forecasts, public spending in 2027 is about €174bn. More than half goes to three areas: pensions, about €40bn, health and social services, about €38bn, and education, about €23bn. Other social security takes about €14bn and defence about €7bn.

Public spending in 2027, estimate (total €174.0bn):

- Pensions €40.2bn, of which earnings-related pensions €37.6bn and national and guarantee pensions (Kela) €2.6bn
- Health and social services (*sote*) €38.2bn
- Education from early childhood education to university €23.2bn
- Other social security €14.4bn
- Defence €7.0bn
- Other spending €51.0bn, of which the largest are transport €8.9bn, municipal and regional administration €7.1bn and interest on debt €6.1bn

Early childhood education is counted under [education](/en/blog/37/education-is-the-municipalitys-most-important-task/). [Health and social services](/en/blog/10/sote-is-the-cornerstone-of-the-welfare-society/) also include Kela's reimbursements for medicines, medical care and travel.

## How much does the public sector borrow, and what does fiscal adjustment mean?

In 2027 public sector spending exceeds revenue by a forecast €13.7bn, or 4.5% of gross domestic product. The difference is financed with debt. Fiscal adjustment means spending cuts, tax increases or a combination of the two, used to reduce the deficit. According to a [preliminary estimate](https://valtioneuvosto.fi/-/10623/seuraavan-vaalikauden-sopeutustavoite-on-vaativa-mutta-taysin-mahdollinen-saavuttaa) published by the Ministry of Finance in February 2026, the next electoral term requires an adjustment of €8–11bn.

The €8–11bn is about 4.5–6% of estimated public spending in 2027. It is more than defence is estimated to take in 2027 and of the same order as transport spending. Interest on debt is estimated at €6.1bn in 2027, almost as much as defence. According to the [Ministry of Finance press release](https://valtioneuvosto.fi/-/10623/seuraavan-vaalikauden-sopeutustavoite-on-vaativa-mutta-taysin-mahdollinen-saavuttaa), the final fiscal position target is to be set in a parliamentary process in December 2026. The target is linked to the [debt brake](/en/blog/61/green-economic-policy-requires-a-seat-at-the-table/).

Interest spending is rising again. After the recession of the 1990s, the public sector paid at most €4.5bn a year in interest, over 7% of all its spending. In the zero-interest year of 2021, interest took €1.3bn, under one percent of spending. In 2024 the sum was €4.4bn, and in 2027 it is an estimated €6.1bn, more than in any year since 1990. As a share of spending that is about 3.5%, roughly half the peak of the 1990s.

The adjustment is worth discussing on the basis of these figures. The question is where the money comes from: spending cuts, taxes or both.

## Where do the figures come from?

The headline figures are from the Ministry of Finance autumn 2026 Economic Survey (*Taloudellinen katsaus*). The more detailed split of revenue and spending for 2027 is an estimate built on official figures, such as the Statistics Finland national accounts and the central government budget proposal. It is not taken directly from official sources. The figures follow the general government (S13) definition and do not include parishes.

The spending split is based on Statistics Finland's preliminary 2024 data by function (COFOG), scaled up to the 2027 level. The deficit of €13.7bn is the Ministry of Finance's own figure. Calculated from the figures with one decimal, the difference is €13.6bn because of rounding. The interest chart's years 1990–2024 are from Statistics Finland table 12a6 (general government consolidated property expenditure, in practice interest), and the 2027 figure is the estimate described above.
