The price of electricity in winter consists of the price of energy, the grid company's transmission charge and taxes, and with spot-price electricity the energy price varies with supply and demand from one day to the next. A mortgage can be covered with interest-rate protection or a fixed rate for years ahead. A household cannot get the same for electricity, even though a data centre and a factory can. Consumers should be able to sign fixed-price electricity contracts longer than two years if they want to.

## Why does the price of electricity vary so much in Finland?

The price of electricity varies in Finland because the spot price is set every day on the power exchange according to supply and demand. On a windy night there is plenty of electricity on offer and the price falls. On a still, frosty evening demand is at its highest and the price rises. In a fixed-price contract the electricity company bears the cost of this variation and builds the risk into the contract price.

A household's electricity bill has three parts:

- **Energy**: the spot price or the fixed price of the contract, plus the seller's basic fee. According to [Helsingin Sanomat](https://www.hs.fi/politiikka/art-2000012270938.html), spot-price electricity peaked in February, the most expensive month of 2026 up to September, when the average price before tax stayed below 14 cents per kilowatt-hour.
- **Transmission**: the transmission charge and basic fee of the local grid company. The transmission price does not follow the exchange, and it is supervised by the Energy Authority (_Energiavirasto_). I wrote about grid ownership and regulation in [Finland Can Turn Electrification Into a Competitive Edge](/en/blog/65/finland-can-turn-electrification-into-a-competitive-edge/).
- **Taxes**: the [electricity tax](https://www.vero.fi/yritykset-ja-yhteisot/tietoa-yritysverotuksesta/valmisteverotus/sahko_ja_eraat_polttoaineet/), which for a household, including the security of supply fee, is about 2.3 cents per kilowatt-hour, and value added tax on the whole bill.

## Why can't electricity be bought at a fixed price for a longer period?

Under the Electricity Market Act (_sähkömarkkinalaki_), a consumer may terminate a fixed-term electricity contract lasting more than two years after two years, in the same way as an open-ended contract. The law does not prohibit a long contract. However, it is difficult for a seller to hedge its price against a customer who can leave midway, so households are generally offered a fixed price for no more than about two years.

The rule is written into the [Electricity Market Act](https://www.finlex.fi/fi/lainsaadanto/2013/588), and the [Energy Authority](https://energiavirasto.fi/-/sahkonostajan-oikeudet) describes it in its guidance on electricity buyers' rights. Its intent is sound, because nobody is left tied for years to a contract they regret. At the same time it takes away a household's chance of long-term predictability.

Businesses do this all the time. They sign long power purchase agreements with electricity producers, known by the abbreviation PPA. For example, [Google signed a 22-year agreement with Fortum](https://www.fortum.com/fi/media/2026/09/sisapiiritieto-fortum-ja-google-vauhdittavat-kestavaa-kasvua-suomessa-yhtiot-ovat-allekirjoittaneet-ydinvoimaa-koskevan-sahkonostosopimuksen-ppa). A factory and a data centre can therefore lock in the price of electricity for years. A household is one of the few buyers that in practice cannot.

## So what should be done?

Consumers should be able to sign fixed-price electricity contracts longer than two years if they want to, in the same way as businesses do. The law would not set the length of the contract. If someone wants to pay more now to secure their price for, say, ten years, that is their own choice.

> A household would get the same protection against price spikes as a data centre and industry, without taxpayers' money.

This is a market-based measure with no subsidies and no price cap. It removes the restriction that prevents a consumer from committing to a fixed price for more than two years. A price cap is not the answer, as I wrote in [Stability Brings Investment, Erratic Policy Drives It Away](/en/blog/77/electricity-market-google-price-cap/).

A fixed price is insurance, and insurance has a price. A long contract does not promise cheaper electricity, because the seller builds the risk into the contract in the same way as a bank does with interest-rate protection. The household buys peace of mind, that is, a known price years ahead. If a long contract were available, short price spikes would no longer weigh as heavily on a household's decisions.

Existing protection would remain. Short contracts, open-ended contracts and spot-price electricity would stay as they are, and the two-year right of termination would still be the default. A buyer committing for longer would have to see the full price and all the terms before signing.

## How do data centres affect the price of electricity?

Data centres affect a household's electricity price above all through the grid. According to a [report](https://wiki.helsinki.fi/xwiki/bin/view/DatakeskuksetCS/) by researchers at the University of Helsinki, Finland's data centres used about 285 megawatts of power in 2025, excluding crypto mining, while Finland's peak consumption is about 15,000 megawatts. The bottleneck is the grid connection. Over one year, Fingrid has received consumption connection enquiries [amounting to about 68 gigawatts](https://www.sttinfo.fi/tiedote/72200778/fingrid-konsernin-puolivuosikatsaus-11-3062026?lang=fi).

The enquiries do not bind anyone, but they show how many want to connect to the same grid. The Government is right that the connection queue has to be cleared. In the [proposal that was out for consultation](https://tem.fi/hanke?tunnus=TEM043%3A00%2F2026), applicants would be placed in priority groups according to purpose of use, and [data centres would face stricter conditions](https://yle.fi/a/74-20248277) than others to qualify for priority.

A better and more market-based solution would be for household and small-business connections to continue to be processed immediately, while the capacity of large connections would be allocated by auction, under the same rules for every sector. A project that commits to flexing its consumption during peak hours would gain an advantage in the auction. The auction proceeds would be used to strengthen the grid, so that the bill would not be collected through households' transmission charges. I wrote about the local effects of a data centre in [my post on Microsoft's data centre project](/en/blog/93/microsoft-data-centre-kirkkonummi/).

## How can a household prepare for winter price spikes now?

A household can prepare for winter price spikes by deciding whether to bear the price risk itself or pay the seller to bear it. A fixed-price contract keeps the energy price the same for the whole contract period. With spot-price electricity the price follows the exchange, and savings come from shifting consumption to cheaper hours. Both are sensible choices once you know which risk you want to bear.

With spot-price electricity, demand flexibility means something like this in everyday life:

- The water heater is controlled by the spot price, so the water is heated during the cheapest hours.
- Laundry, dishwashing and electric car charging are timed for the night or other low-price hours.

When consumption moves away from peak hours, the consumption peak flattens and the peak price stays lower for everyone. A holder of a fixed contract should check the contract's end date, so that a new contract can be chosen before the old one expires.

In Parliament I would push for households to get the same opportunity for a long fixed price as industry and data centres. The length of the contract would then be decided by the buyer.
